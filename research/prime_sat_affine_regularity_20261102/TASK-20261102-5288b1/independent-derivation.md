**0. Convention and primary-source boundary.**

For a finite polynomial list H in a standard graded polynomial ring P over a field, omit identically zero rows when forming H_top, and let I_H be the ideal generated by the highest homogeneous parts of the other rows. When P/I_H is finite-dimensional, use

    dreg(H) = min { d >= 0 : (P/I_H)_d = 0 }.

This is the degree-of-regularity convention in Caminata–Gorla, Definition 1.4, PDF page 6. Definition 1.1, PDF page 4, defines a different invariant using the degree at which the specified Macaulay procedure contains a Gröbner basis. No equality with that solving degree is assumed here. These are the only external mathematical definitions used; the chart and reduction arguments below are independently derived. [Primary reference, version 2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2112.05579v2).

The zero quotient has dreg 0. The ring K with no variables and no nonzero equations has degree-zero component K and all positive components zero, hence dreg 1. In a polynomial ring with at least one variable the zero ideal has an infinite-dimensional quotient, so the stated finite-dimensional definition does not assign it a finite dreg. A homogeneous quotient of a standard graded polynomial ring has no nonzero components after its first zero component: every monomial in the next degree is a variable times a monomial in the preceding degree. Thus for a nonzero finite-dimensional quotient, dreg is one plus its largest nonzero degree.

The ordinary ideal (H), the ideal (H_top), and the ideal of highest parts of *all* members of (H) are different objects. This derivation uses the second. In particular, an inconsistent ordinary system need not have dreg 0 unless its input highest-part ideal is already the unit ideal.

**1. Joint graded_quotient: the graded surjection.**

Let R=K[x,z_1,...,z_n], S=K[z_1,...,z_n], n>=0, let ell be homogeneous linear in z, and let c be a scalar. Put

    L = x - ell - c,
    F = (L, f_1, ..., f_m),
    psi(f) = f(ell+c,z),
    G = (psi(f_i) : psi(f_i) != 0),
    I = (F_top), J = (G_top).

There are two maps to keep distinct: psi is the affine evaluation map; phi:R->S, given by phi(x)=ell and phi(z_i)=z_i, is graded and surjective. Its kernel is (x-ell), and phi(L_top)=0.

For a nonzero f_i of degree d, the degree-d homogeneous component of psi(f_i) is exactly phi((f_i)_top). To see this term by term, replacing x by ell+c in a monomial of total degree d produces degree-d terms only by choosing ell at every occurrence of x. Terms of f_i of degree less than d cannot produce degree d. Consequently:

- If phi((f_i)_top) is nonzero, psi(f_i) has degree d and its highest part is precisely phi((f_i)_top), a generator of J.
- If phi((f_i)_top)=0, that generator maps to zero, whether psi(f_i) vanishes, becomes a nonzero constant, or has some positive degree less than d. Any new highest part exposed by the degree drop is still retained in G.
- If f_i is a nonzero scalar, its image is that same scalar and J is the unit ideal.

Every generator of I therefore maps into J. The composite R --phi--> S -> S/J factors through a graded surjection

    R/I  --->  S/J.

Equivalently, since x-ell belongs to I,

    R/I = S/H,    H = (phi((f_i)_top)),    H subseteq J,

where the displayed equality denotes the induced graded isomorphism. The inclusion is in this direction; newly exposed lower-degree rows can enlarge J relative to H. In every degree d the induced linear map is onto, so

    dim_K(S/J)_d <= dim_K(R/I)_d.

If R/I is finite-dimensional, so is S/J. Its first zero component occurs no later, and dreg(G)<=dreg(F). This proves the conditional non-strict claim over every field.

The proof includes ell=0 and c arbitrary. It includes vanished rows: their degree-d images are zero, and their omission changes neither solutions nor J. It also includes a nonzero scalar arising only after substitution. For example, with L=x-1 and f=x, the original highest-part quotient is K, while the reduced row is 1 and the reduced quotient is zero. Here dreg falls from 1 to 0; treating that scalar as a vanished row would be wrong.

For n=0, ell=0 and S=K. Every substituted row is a scalar. If all vanish, G is empty and J=0; the map has target K and its dreg is 1. If any is nonzero, J=S and dreg is 0. If G is empty but n>0, S/J is infinite-dimensional, so finite dimensionality of R/I is impossible by the surjection. A nonzero constant input gives unit ideals on both sides. These cover the zero ideal, unit ideal, empty list, and no-remaining-variable boundaries without changing the convention.

Affine substitution also cannot increase an individual row's total degree. This is a rowwise statement, including degree drops to a scalar and disappearance of a zero row; no maximum-degree convention for an empty list is needed.

**2. Joint solution_field_equations: reconstruction and Frobenius.**

Let E/K be any field extension. If (x,z) satisfies F over E, its L row forces x=ell(z)+c, and each other equation becomes psi(f_i)(z)=0. Conversely, if z satisfies every retained row of G, define x=ell(z)+c. Then L and every f_i vanish; a discarded zero row imposes no condition. Projection and this reconstruction are inverse maps. This proves a bijection over each extension, including empty solution sets and the unique empty tuple in zero variables when it is a solution. No division is involved.

Now let K=F_p, ell=sum_i a_i z_i, with a_i,c in F_p. The characteristic-p binomial identity and a_i^p=a_i, c^p=c give the polynomial identity

    psi(x^p-x)
      = (ell+c)^p-(ell+c)
      = sum_i a_i (z_i^p-z_i).                         (2.1)

Suppose every surviving variable z_i, including every auxiliary, retains its field equation z_i^p-z_i. Equation (2.1) certifies ordinary-ideal redundancy of the substituted x field row. If ell!=0, its degree is p and its highest part is sum_i a_i z_i^p, which is in the linear span of the highest parts of those retained field rows. If ell=0, (2.1) is the zero polynomial. Hence deleting this particular transformed row preserves both the ordinary ideal and the highest-part ideal. For n=0 it is also zero. If some a_i=0, the corresponding field row is not needed for this identity, but it is still needed in the general all-variable field augmentation unless independently justified.

More generally, after substitution a nonzero row q may be deleted without changing the highest-part ideal exactly when q_top lies in the ideal of the highest parts of the remaining rows. If solutions or the ordinary ideal are also to be preserved, a separate corresponding certificate is needed; highest-part membership alone does not imply ordinary redundancy. Having all z_i field rows is a convenient sufficient condition for both certificates for (2.1), not a necessary condition in every specially constrained system.

All degree boundaries are explicit. The pivot is always a degree-one equation because its x coefficient is 1, even if its right side is constant. An arbitrary linear row after substitution is retained if it becomes a nonzero linear row or a nonzero scalar, and omitted only if it becomes zero. The transformed field row in (2.1) is either degree p or zero; it is not silently replaced by a degree-one equation. A scalar reconstruction over F_p makes that field row zero.

Two controls expose missing premises.

First, omit a surviving field equation. In F_p[x,z], take F=(x-z,x^p-x) with no z field row. Substitution gives G=(z^p-z). Its highest ideal is (z^p) and its dreg is p. Deleting this row would leave F_p[z], which has no finite dreg in this convention. Over an extension containing elements outside F_p the deleted row also changes the solution set. Calling every variable a field variable without actually retaining its polynomial field equation does not repair either issue.

Second, work in a field K of characteristic p containing a not in F_p. For x=a z, the transformed field row is

    a^p z^p-a z = a^p(z^p-z) + (a^p-a)z.

At z=1, the surviving field row vanishes but this row does not. Thus the usual redundancy claim fails. Its highest part can nevertheless lie in (z^p), illustrating why a highest-part certificate is not an ordinary-ideal certificate. In full generality the missing terms are

    (ell+c)^p-(ell+c)
      = sum_i a_i^p(z_i^p-z_i)
        + sum_i(a_i^p-a_i)z_i + (c^p-c).

A constant c outside F_p already gives a nonzero scalar obstruction when ell=0. None of these counterexamples is within the contract's F_p coefficient premise.

If a presentation in N variables explicitly includes all N field equations, its highest-part ideal contains every v_i^p. The quotient is spanned by the monomials with each exponent at most p-1 and is finite-dimensional. A nonzero quotient then has dreg at most N(p-1)+1; the unit quotient has dreg 0. This argument also gives dreg 1 for the empty list in N=0 variables. It is a symbolic spanning argument, not a census. Field augmentation has input degree p whenever a field variable remains, even when all chart arithmetic rows have degree at most two.

Full-field substitution cannot simply be substituted for Boolean-bit reasoning. Over F_p with p>3, impose x^2-x=y^2-y=z^2-z=0 and x=y+z. After eliminating x, its idempotence equation becomes

    (y+z)^2-(y+z) = (y^2-y)+(z^2-z)+2yz.

The two surviving bit equations allow y=z=1, but the omitted equation then equals 2, and reconstructed x=2 is not Boolean. The transformed idempotence guard is necessary. Characteristic two with coefficients in F_2 is a different case. The current canonical range compilation has its own value bits and recurrence states; a full-field affine formula does not itself authenticate a bit-level replacement.

**3. Joint row_retention: ordinary redundancy is insufficient.**

    H = (z^2+z, z^2, z),    H' = (z^2+z, z^2).

Every displayed row is divisible by z, and (z^2+z)-z^2=z. Thus (H)=(H')=(z). But

    (H_top)=(z),       (H'_top)=(z^2).

The quotients have respective monomial bases {1} and {1,z}. Their Hilbert polynomials are 1 and 1+t, and dreg(H)=1 while dreg(H')=2. Ordinary-ideal equality therefore does not determine this input-presentation invariant.

A zero row has no highest part and may be omitted. The transformed x field row has both certificates from part 2.     q^epsilon v^i w^j,   epsilon in {0,1}, 0<=i,j<=p-1

    H_F(t) = (1+t)(1+t+...+t^(p-1))^2.

The monomial q v^(p-1) w^(p-1) is nonzero of degree 2p-1, and there is no basis monomial of higher degree. Hence dreg(F)=2p.

    J = (q^2, v+2bq, q^p, v^p, w^p).                 (4.2)

    J_0 = (q^2, v, w^p).

    q^epsilon w^j,   epsilon in {0,1}, 0<=j<=p-1,

and Hilbert polynomial (1+t)(1+t+...+t^(p-1)). Its last nonzero degree is p, witnessed by q w^(p-1).

If b!=0, put M=(q^2,v+2bq,w^p). The three generators of M occur in (4.2), so M subseteq J. In the reverse direction q^p belongs to (q^2), and

    v^p = (v+2bq)^p - (2b)^p q^p

    F_p[q,v,w]/J  =  F_p[q,w]/(q^2,w^p).

    dreg(G)=p+1 < 2p=dreg(F).
