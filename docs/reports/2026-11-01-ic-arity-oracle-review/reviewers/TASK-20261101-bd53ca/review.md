## Explicit breaking artifacts

**F1: minimum versus infimum, required m=2,k=2 control.** For 0<β≤1,

```text
C(2,β,2)=max(1−β+2β,2β)=1+β>1.
```

For β≥1, `C(2,β,2)=2β≥2`. Thus `inf_{β>0} C(2,β,2)=1` as β→0+, but there is no minimizer. The target's k≥m−1 clause applies and its blanket attainment assertion fails. More generally, for k>m−1, the unsaturated term is `1+β(k−m+1)>1`, while the saturated region also has cost>1; the infimum is 1 and is not attained. At k=m−1, sufficiently small positive β with β≤min(1/(m−1),1/2) attains 1. No new Lean counterexample was compiled; this is an explicit real-arithmetic derivation.

**F2: the middle lower bound need not be attained.** With m=5,k=0 the claimed middle bound is 0, but `C≥2β>0`, and the small-bound declarations give minimum 1/3 at β=1/6. The source note itself restricts the middle attainment to k≥2; the frozen target loses this restriction.

**F3: pointwise threshold wording is false.** At m=5,k=1,β=1, the right-hand condition m>k+3 holds but C=2. At β=1/5 the cost is 2/5. Therefore the same (m,k) supports both sides of 1/2 as β varies. The intended optimized threshold can hold while the literal pointwise iff fails. At m=7,k=2,β=1, the large-order condition also holds but C=2. The two threshold declarations only compare their rational expressions with 1/2.

**F4: strictness in the producer note and a Lean doc comment.** Note §3(6) and the comment preceding `costOne_ge_enum` say the one-return value is strictly worse for k≥2,m≥3. In the common optimum range,

```text

```

At m=3,k=2 both optima equal 1, attained for example at β=1/3 for D and β=1/2 for C. Strictness needs k<m−1. Note §4's “no enumerative oracle of order k≥(m−1)/2 reaches the generic exponent” also needs a wording correction: at m=5,k=2,β=1/4, C=1/2. “Never below” is compatible with that boundary; “strictly above” or “cannot reach” is not.

A mean does not supply the one-return success law in note §2. For an abstract nonnegative count Y, take `Y=L` with probability μ/L and zero otherwise, with L≥μ. Then E[Y]=μ but Pr[Y>0]=μ/L; this can be far below min(1,μ). This is an underdetermination witness for the inference, not a claimed curve construction. A Poisson/random-occupancy approximation would need additional justification and uniform tail control. Likewise B returned relation rows can all be duplicates and have rank one. Collecting a number of decompositions is not collecting independent rank.

For all-return semantics the mean can motivate a workload estimate, but using it to predict the stopping time to useful rank needs more than a conservation law. For one-return semantics, even “at most one” does not ensure that an existing decomposition will be found. `C≤D` compares the two definitions with the same k and β; it does not compare two actual solvers with different k, setup, success distributions or memory.

Output cost is also an interface: if an all-return call explicitly emits Y decompositions, its charge must include that output. At exponent level, expected output size may impose `kβ≥max(0,mβ−1)` before factorial corrections (and appropriate tuple-length/bit costs). The unrestricted real formulas admit parameters not realizable by an explicit-output oracle under that cost convention. A compressed representation requires a separate decoding/rank accounting. This does not invalidate the formal definitions or the listed admissible optimum examples.

The exact multiset-count mean cited in the note is `binom(B+m−1,m)/r` under uniform targets. Its conservation does not imply occupancy distribution or independent rank. An alleged increase above that same exact uniform mean would have to change the target distribution, counting convention, or base/sample assumptions; it cannot be attributed to base structure while retaining an identical conservation identity.

## Growing arity and source scale

Use the PFSE scale `n=ceil(log2 r)`, with `log2 p=Theta(n)`. A positive fixed exponent, even one below 1/2, is still exponential in n. A claim of `2^o(n)` requires complete phase costs whose normalized logarithms tend to zero along an admissible schedule `m(n),β(n),k(n)`, plus a uniform algorithm and success guarantee. The Lean file contains neither n nor such a limit.

For fixed m, m! and polynomial-in-n factors may be absorbed into `r^{o(1)}`. Along growing m this needs proof:

```text
delta_factorial(n)=log2(m(n)!)/log2(r).
```

Ignoring the factorial requires `log(m!)=o(n)`, equivalently `m log m=o(n)` for unbounded integer m. At m of order n/log n, it is generally a nonzero exponent contribution. Keeping it changes the idealized attempt terms to approximately

```text
all: max(0,1+delta_factorial−β(m−1)),
one: β+max(0,1+delta_factorial−βm),
```

subject still to the success/rank assumptions. Moreover,

```text
binom(B+m−1,m)=(B^m/m!) product_{j=0}^{m−1}(1+j/B).
```

The logarithm of that product also needs a uniform o(n) bound; `m²/B=o(n)` is a sufficient bound for that logarithmic purpose, while the stronger `m²/B→0` supports a ratio approximation. Neither is proved here. Integer rounding of B and m, row length m, field-operation bit costs, equation degree/representation size, and every m-dependent hidden constant need the same treatment. “For every fixed m, overhead is r^{o(1)}” does not imply this along m=m(n).

Two scope checks expose what the algebra can and cannot exclude. For integer m(n) tending to infinity, with k=1 and β=1/m, the formal optimum is 2/m→0. For example m=floor(sqrt(n)) eventually satisfies the attainment hypotheses and has log(m!)=o(n). This is an admitted formula schedule with unimplemented solver/rank/descent obligations, not an algorithm. In contrast, k≥m/2 gives C>1/2 for every n in the model, so its normalized model exponent cannot tend to zero; the margin above 1/2 may shrink as m grows. The theorem supplies no uniform positive margin above 1/2, concrete-size speed comparison, or lower bound for an algorithm not shown to obey this model.
